STAGE 1: CONTEXT ENGINEERING

How Watching History Turns Into a Language Prompt

What is happening here? (Plain English)

Traditional recommender systems turn your watch history into a list of numbers (like [0, 1, 0, 0, 4.2]). This is called "tabular feature engineering". The problem? Numbers throw away the story! They forget that you watched the finale twice, or that you prefer dark thrillers specifically late at night on your TV.

GenRec's breakthrough is to keep everything in natural language. It writes a structured story describing your recent sessions and lets a language model read it like a human would.

Technical Formulation

In traditional systems, features are engineered as sparse categorical crosses and aggregated continuous summaries. GenRec formalizes context through a deterministic verbalization mapping from viewing interaction sequences into structured natural language sequences.

STAGE 2: SERVING EFFICIENCY

Why GenRec Uses "Prefill-Only" Instead of Normal Text Generation

The Latency & Hallucination Problem (Plain English)

When you use ChatGPT or Claude, the AI writes out words one-by-one. That takes 1 to 2 seconds. If Netflix took 2 seconds to load your homepage every time you clicked a row, the app would feel broken. Furthermore, a text-generating AI might make up a movie that doesn't exist, or recommend a movie Netflix doesn't own!

How GenRec fixes this: GenRec completely disables word generation. It looks at your prompt in a single step (the prefill pass), generates a mathematical fingerprint of your taste, and compares it to all movies in the catalog at once. This takes only 15 milliseconds and guarantees 0% hallucinations.

Serving Complexity Formulation

Autoregressive generation incurs computational complexity O(N · L) where N is generated token length and L is context length, requiring N sequential memory round-trips. Prefill-only scoring operates in O(1) decode steps by taking the prompt sequence hidden state and projecting onto candidate catalog embeddings.

STAGE 3: EMPIRICAL SCALING LAWS

Why GenRec Needs 40× Less Data Than Traditional Models

What does "Sample Efficiency" and "MRR" mean? (Plain English)

What is MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank)?
MRR is the standard way to grade recommendation systems. Think of it like a search engine grade: If the movie you actually wanted to watch was placed in the #1 slot, the score is 1.0 (an A+). If it was in the #2 slot, the score is 0.5. If it was buried way down at #10, the score drops to 0.1.
A higher MRR means the right movie appears right at the top of your screen where you actually see it.

Why does GenRec need 40× less data?
Old machine learning models start out knowing nothing about the real world. They don't know that "space travel" relates to "black holes" until they observe millions of humans clicking both. Because GenRec starts with a pre-trained foundation language model, it already understands language, themes, and narrative concepts. It learns user preferences with a tiny fraction of the data.

Mathematical & Scaling Formulation

Given a set of recommendation queries Q, Mean Reciprocal Rank is the mean of reciprocal ranks. Traditional deep tabular models suffer from high sample complexity due to randomly initialized sparse embedding tables for item IDs. GenRec benefits from transfer learning: foundation model weights provide an informative prior over narrative semantics, drastically reducing sample requirements.

STAGE 02

Two-Phase Training

Phase 1: Foundation Domain Adaptation on catalog narratives.
Phase 2: Reward-weighted listwise ranking loss optimizing long-term member satisfaction.

Loss = -∑ r_b · log( P(target | u) )
r_b = Reward weight from completion %
STAGE 03

Prefill-Only Production Serving

Processes prompt in a single forward pass. Evaluates dot-product similarity against candidate embeddings in <20 milliseconds.

s(u, c_i) = ϕ(h_u)ᵀ ψ(e_c) / τ
